What the Wood Remembers

For forty years Emory Vaughn has built the same small boat the same way, in the same shed above Sennet Cove. On one long afternoon in October we watched him begin another — and learned that almost nothing about it is quick, and almost nothing about it is decided in advance.

Words by Nora Ellison  ·  Photographs by Perrin Doyle

Emory Vaughn keeps the same length of chalk in the same coat pocket he has kept it in for longer than most of the boats on this reach have been afloat. He is seventy-one. The shed sits a hundred yards above Sennet Cove on a slope that has never been level, propped at its downhill corner on a granite block his father set there, and from the wide door you can see the whole working water of the cove: two lobster boats on their moorings, a skiff hauled out and turned turtle, and the grey line on the mud where the tide was an hour ago and is not now.

“People think the hard part is the shape,” he says, before he has picked up a single tool. “The shape is the easy part. The shape is decided. The hard part is the wood, because the wood has not agreed to it yet.”

He has built the same hull the same way for forty years, and he still stops to look at the grain before the first cut.

He builds peapods — double-ended rowing and sailing boats, thirteen and fifteen feet, the kind of small workboat that was rowed out to tend traps along this coast a century ago and is now bought, mostly, by people who love the look of them and the quiet of them and the fact that they were made by a pair of hands and not pressed out of a mould in a shed on the other side of the world.

The shape itself lives on the wall of the shed, and it is older than he is. It is a half-model — one side of a hull carved from laminated pine, glued up in stripes of light and dark so that when he wants to know a dimension he can lift the offsets straight off it. His father carved it, and his father’s shape it remains, refined over the decades by nothing more violent than a spokeshave taken to it on the two or three winters when a boat came out and something about her did not sit right in the water.
