HEADLINE: What the Wood Remembers

STANDFIRST: For forty years Emory Vaughn has built the same small boat the same way, in the same shed above Sennet Cove. On one long afternoon in October we watched him begin another - and learned that almost nothing about it is quick, and almost nothing about it is decided in advance. [SET OVER THE OPENING PHOTOGRAPH, on a tint of the picture, so the photograph still reads behind the words.]

BYLINE: Words by Nora Ellison. Photographs by Perrin Doyle.

BODY:

Emory Vaughn keeps the same length of chalk in the same coat pocket he has kept it in for longer than most of the boats on this reach have been afloat. He is seventy-one. The shed sits a hundred yards above Sennet Cove on a slope that has never been level, propped at its downhill corner on a granite block his father set there, and from the wide door you can see the whole working water of the cove: two lobster boats on their moorings, a skiff hauled out and turned turtle, and the grey line on the mud where the tide was an hour ago and is not now.

"People think the hard part is the shape," he says, before he has picked up a single tool. "The shape is the easy part. The shape is decided. The hard part is the wood, because the wood has not agreed to it yet."

He builds peapods - double-ended rowing and sailing boats, thirteen and fifteen feet, the kind of small workboat that was rowed out to tend traps along this coast a century ago and is now bought, mostly, by people who love the look of them and the quiet of them and the fact that they were made by a pair of hands and not pressed out of a mould in a shed on the other side of the world. He has built, by his own uncertain count, "somewhere past three hundred." He does not keep a list. "If I kept a list I would start comparing them," he says, "and they do not compare. Each one is the only one while I am on it."

The shape itself lives on the wall of the shed, and it is older than he is. It is a half-model - one side of a hull carved from laminated pine, glued up in stripes of light and dark so that when he wants to know a dimension he can lift the offsets straight off it. His father carved it, and his father's shape it remains, refined over the decades by nothing more violent than a spokeshave taken to it on the two or three winters when a boat came out and something about her did not sit right in the water. "You do not redesign her," he says of it. "You listen to the ones that swim funny and you take a whisper off the model, and in ten years you have a boat nobody would change." When there is a decision to make he lofts the lines out full size in chalk on a painted patch of the floor, kneeling, sweeping a long limber batten into a curve and pinning it and standing to look, and the loft is where the boat first exists at the size a person will actually sit in.

He has built the same hull the same way for forty years, and he still stops to look at the grain before the first cut. That is the thing you notice first, watching him: how much of the work is looking. He picks up a cedar board from the rack along the back wall, carries it to the door where the light is, and turns it, and turns it again, and holds it up so the low sun rakes across the surface and throws the grain into relief. He is reading it the way you would read a face. "This one wants to be a garboard," he says at last, of the plank that runs first and lowest against the keel, the one that takes the hardest twist. "You can see it wants to go round. You do not fight a board that wants to go round."

The backbone comes first - the keel, the stem, the sternpost - cut from oak, heavier and darker than the cedar that will skin it. He sets it up on the trestles in the middle of the floor and stands back from it, and for a while he does nothing at all but look, walking around it, crouching to sight along its length with one eye shut. "Fair," he says, which is the word that runs through everything he does. A line is fair when there is no place your eye catches on it, no flat, no bump, nothing that argues. You cannot measure fair. You can only see it, and he has spent fifty years teaching his eye to see it, and he does not entirely trust anyone else's.

Before any of it, though, come the tools, and he sharpens them the way other men say grace. The first half-hour of the day is a stone and a strop and not one board touched, and he will stop in the middle of the afternoon and go back to the stone if a shaving comes off ragged. "A dull tool does not cut cedar, it tears it," he says, "and a torn surface never takes finish right, and you will see it for the life of the boat." His plane irons are ground to a camber only he can read; his chisels are his father's, the boxwood handles worn to the shape of a hand that is not quite the shape of his own. He has bought exactly two new tools in thirty years. Everything else he keeps alive.

The planking is where the boat becomes a boat. Each plank is spiled - shaped so that its edge lands sweetly against the one below it - and then it has to be persuaded round the curve of the hull, which is where the steam box comes in. He fires it up on an old propane ring in the corner, and when the box is roaring he feeds a plank in and waits, and while he waits he does not fill the time. He stands. "Ten minutes to the inch," he says, of the timber's thickness, and he times it by feel more than by clock. When it comes out it comes out fast and hot and pliable and there is a short, urgent window in which two people bend it home and clamp it before it sets, and for those few minutes the quiet shed is all motion and short words, and then it is over and the plank is on and cooling into its new shape and he is looking at it again.

"A boat is not a drawing," he says, wiping his hands. "A drawing holds still. Water does not. So the boat cannot hold still either. It has to give a little everywhere, and to give the same everywhere, or it will find the one place you were mean with it and it will work there until it lets the water in."

The fastenings are copper - rivets and roves, set by hand, one person holding an iron on the head while the other peens the rove down over the shank with a light hammer, tap tap tap, a sound that carries out the open door and across the cove. He counts them in his head. A fifteen-foot boat takes upward of a thousand. He has driven, he supposes, some hundreds of thousands in his life, and he still lines each one up as if it were the first, because a rivet set crooked is a rivet that will weep. "Nobody sees the ones you get right," he says. "They only ever see the one you got wrong."

A boat like this takes him the best part of four months and he asks for it what a good used car costs, and by any honest reckoning of the hours that is far less than nothing. There is a wait - a little over a year now - and he keeps it in a school notebook by the door, one name to a page, and he telephones people himself when their turn comes because he does not use the computer his niece set up for him. The people who wait are not, mostly, people who need a boat. "They could buy a plastic one on a Tuesday and be rowing it on a Wednesday," he says, without scorn. "They are not waiting for a boat. They are waiting for this boat. I do not fully understand it and I am not going to argue them out of it." He is aware, in a way that plainly costs him something to say, that the thing he makes has quietly become a luxury, when it began its life as the cheapest way a lobsterman could get out to his traps. "That is the world's doing, not mine," he says. "I just kept making the same boat until the world changed around it."

He learned from his father, in this shed, and his father learned from a man in a shed two coves east who is long dead and whose name Emory still says with care. There is a line running back through those sheds that he is aware of holding, and it troubles him, quietly, that he can see the end of it from where he stands. He has had apprentices. Two stayed a season and left for money, which he does not begrudge them - "there is no money in this, there was never any money in this" - and one stayed three years and was good, genuinely good, and then moved inland to raise a family, which he begrudges nobody either. "You cannot ask a young person to be poor and patient at the same time," he says. "That is two hard things."

But he is not sentimental about it, or he does not let himself be for long. When you ask him whether it matters that the skill might not outlast him, he looks at the boat on the trestles for a while before he answers. "It matters to me," he says. "Whether it matters is not really my department. My department is this one. If I get this one right, that is the whole of what I can do about it."

You do not force cedar. You ask it, and then you wait to hear what it says back. He says something close to that late in the afternoon, when the sun has come round to the door and the shavings on the floor are lit up gold and the day's work is nearly done, and he is not performing it, he is just describing how the work goes, and it is the truest thing anyone could tell you about him. There is no hurry in the shed and there never has been. The boat will be finished when it is fair and fastened and not before, and it will go into the water in the spring, and someone will row it out onto a cove somewhere, and it will give a little everywhere, the same everywhere, and it will not let the water in.

He walks us to the door at the end. The tide has turned and is coming back up the mud. He looks at it the way he looks at everything, without hurry, reading it. "Same water," he says. "Every day. It does not get bored of it and neither do I." Then he goes back in to sweep the floor, because you always sweep the floor, because a clean floor is a sign that the day was done properly, and he has done every day properly for forty years, and he intends to do this one properly too.
